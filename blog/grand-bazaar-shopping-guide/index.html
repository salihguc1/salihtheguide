The Grand Bazaar: how to shop and bargain without stress

When to go, how bargaining works, how to judge a fair price, what's worth buying, and how not to get lost in Istanbul's Grand Bazaar.

10 November 2026 · 6 min read · By Salih Güç

The Grand Bazaar is one of the oldest and largest covered markets in the world, with thousands of shops along dozens of covered streets. It can feel overwhelming on a first visit. My advice is to go with one thing in mind to look for, and an hour or two to look around. A towel, a small bowl or a scarf is enough to get you comparing shops and talking to sellers. And you can enjoy the bazaar even if you leave empty-handed.

When to go and where to enter

The Grand Bazaar, Kapalıçarşı in Turkish, is open from Monday to Saturday and closed on Sundays. It also closes for the main religious holidays, and some individual shops keep shorter hours than the bazaar itself. Check the current opening hours before you go. If shopping is the main reason for your visit, go in the morning, when it’s calmer.

There are two easy ways in:

  • Beyazıt Gate, on the west side: take the T1 tram to Beyazıt–Kapalıçarşı, a few steps away.
  • Nuruosmaniye Gate, on the east side: get off the tram one stop earlier, at Çemberlitaş, or walk from Sultanahmet.

Save the name of your gate on your phone before you turn into the smaller lanes.

Finding your way around

The main street inside, Kalpakçılar Caddesi, runs straight between the Beyazıt and Nuruosmaniye gates and is lined with jewellery shops. Use it as your reference point. If you get lost, head back to it, or ask Beyazıt Kapısı nerede? (“Where’s the Beyazıt Gate?”). Shopkeepers are used to the question.

How to bargain without it getting awkward

Start with Bu ne kadar? (“How much is this?”). If the price is given in euros or dollars, ask Türk lirası olarak ne kadar? (“How much is it in Turkish lira?”) so you can compare shops. Make sure the price is for the exact piece in your hand, and whether it’s for one item or a set.

Some shops have fixed prices. If you’re not sure, ask Fiyat sabit mi? (“Is the price fixed?”). If there’s room to negotiate, ask for a better price once you’ve seen something similar in another shop: Biraz indirim olur mu? (“Could you give me a small discount?”). Buying more than one helps. For towels, for example, try İki tane alırsam indirim olur mu? (“Is there a discount if I buy two?”).

There’s no magic percentage that works for every purchase. A big discount only tells you how far the seller moved from their first price. It doesn’t tell you anything about the quality, or what the same thing costs next door. Only make an offer once you’re sure you’d be happy to buy at that price, because it’s considered rude to agree on a price and then walk away.

If the price still feels too high, say Teşekkürler, biraz bakınacağım (“Thanks, I’ll look around a bit”) and move on. If you think you might come back, ask for the shop’s card, because finding the same doorway again from memory is harder than it sounds.

How to judge whether a price is fair

Compare two or three similar items before deciding. With towels, look at the size, the fabric and how tightly they’re woven. With ceramics, look at the finish and ask how the piece was made. Two bowls that look the same across the counter can turn out to be quite different in quality and origin.

I feel comfortable buying when the seller can explain what I’m getting, tells me the total price in lira, and gives me time to decide. For more expensive items, ask for a written description and a receipt. If shipping comes up, ask for the item price, the shipping cost and the delivery terms separately before paying.

  • Check the currency on the card machine. Make sure the amount is the one you agreed and that it’s shown in TRY. My post on Istanbul scams explains why.
  • Check the item before it’s wrapped. Unfold the towel, look inside the bowl, hold the scarf up to the light.
  • Take your time with expensive purchases. If you’re buying a carpet or jewellery, leave the shop, look at another option, and come back if it’s still the one you want.

What to buy on a first visit

A peştamal, the thin, flat-woven cotton towel used in hamams, is a great first buy. It’s light, packs easily, and you can feel the fabric before deciding. Ask what it’s made of and whether the price is for one towel or a bundle. A small ceramic dish is another good choice, as long as the shop can wrap it well enough for your flight home.

If a seller says a bowl is hand-painted or a scarf is silk, ask them to show you what makes that piece different from the cheaper ones. If you plan to eat from a ceramic, ask whether it’s made for food or for decoration. In the end, buy the piece you like at a price you’re happy with, especially when you can’t check a claim about how or where it was made.

Carpets are worth looking at even if you’re just curious. If you’re seriously thinking of buying one, measure the room at home before your trip, and ask about the size, materials, how it was made and where it comes from.

Beyond the covered streets: the hans

A han is an old commercial building with shops and workshops arranged around a courtyard. Many of these are still working buildings, and they show a side of the bazaar most visitors miss. Inside the bazaar, look for Zincirli Han. Outside, explore the streets to the north around Mahmutpaşa and Çakmakçılar Yokuşu, where you’ll find Büyük Valide Han, one of the largest in the city.

People work in these buildings, so if a courtyard is open, have a quiet look from the entrance and keep your visit short. Don’t go into upper floors or onto roofs unless it’s clearly allowed.

From Mahmutpaşa, the shopping streets slope downhill all the way to Eminönü, the Spice Bazaar and the ferry piers. It’s a good route out if you want to finish by the water, but the slope and the crowds slow you down, so leave time for a tea stop. For ideas near the waterfront, see What to Do in İstanbul.
